A book I love

The Mysterious Case of Rudolf Diesel

Genius, Power, and Deception on the Eve of World War I

by Douglas Brunt

The Story

On the night of September 29, 1913, Rudolf Diesel — the inventor of the engine that still bears his name — boarded the steamship Dresden to cross the English Channel to London. By morning he was gone. His cabin bed hadn't been slept in, his coat was folded neatly by the rail, and days later a body was pulled from the sea.

Douglas Brunt reconstructs Diesel's extraordinary life and the world around him: the race between oil barons and coal, the naval arms build-up between Britain and Germany, and the powerful men who had reasons to want Diesel silenced. Then he weighs the theories — suicide, murder, or something far stranger — and makes a compelling case of his own.

Why I Recommend It

  1. 1

    It reads like a thriller

    It's nonfiction, but the pacing, suspense, and cast of characters make it as gripping as a spy novel. It's hard to put down.

  2. 2

    A look inside the engine

    It gives a deep introspection into the origins and functionality of diesel engines.

  3. 3

    A real unsolved mystery

    Brunt lays out the evidence fairly and lets you play detective before revealing his own theory — and it's a surprising one.

A Favorite Quotation

“Our goal should not be to better the future happiness of men through intolerance and externals. Rather we should help our brothers on this earth, improve the situation of mankind, and redress poverty to the best of our ability.”

— Rudolf Diesel

My Go-To Recommendation

I once got talking with a fellow power-plant-obsessed weirdo, and the conversation drifted to liquefied coal. That was my cue to bring up this book.

One of my favorite details in it is that Rudolf Diesel actually wanted the British to run on liquefied coal instead of gasoline. Britain didn't need foreign oil for it. The fuel could be produced from the coal mines they already had.

Made with admiration for a great read.
